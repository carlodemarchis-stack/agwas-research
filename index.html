Research

I research to do my job. I advise companies in sports and media technology, and I write about the same market, and neither holds up on instinct. So I count things: who pitched what, which claims repeat across a show floor, what a category looks like once it is laid out in rows.

Most of it stays on my machine. Some is covered by what clients tell me in confidence. Some is thin, built in an afternoon to settle one question, and of no use to anyone who was not in the room. That work stays where it is, and you are not missing much.

Every so often a file gets far enough that it might be worth someone else's time. This is not analyst-grade work. The firms that do it properly have staff, panels and a method I cannot match, and I have an inbox and some evenings. There is still something useful below that line, as long as the file says out loud how it was built. This hub is where those land.

So each one carries its dates, its sources and the shape of its sample. Where a record rests on a subject line instead of the full message, it says so. Where a call is weak, it says that too. Read the method section first: an inbox is a record of who spent money on public relations, so a scan built from mine over-represents vendors with agencies and under-represents everyone else.
